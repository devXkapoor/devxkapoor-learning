Node 8 — time: the clock, and every lie you were told about it

The limitation: the scheduler needs a periodic interrupt and an accurate, cheap, monotonic measure of elapsed time, and neither has been built.

Where a computer gets time at all

There are several sources and they are not interchangeable.

A crystal oscillator — a piece of quartz that vibrates at a fixed frequency when voltage is applied — is the physical basis. Divide its frequency down and you have a pulse at a known rate. Every clock in the machine descends from one of these, and each one is slightly wrong: a typical crystal is accurate to perhaps 20 parts per million, which is about 1.7 seconds of drift per day, and its rate changes measurably with temperature. Two computers left alone will disagree, and the disagreement grows. That is the root fact behind everything in the distributed-systems part of this node.

On top of that, several devices expose time in different ways:

  • The RTC (real-time clock) is a small battery-backed chip that keeps date and time while the machine is off. It is read exactly once, at boot, to initialise the system clock. It is coarse — one-second resolution — and it is the reason a machine with a dead motherboard battery boots believing it is 2010.
  • The PIT (8253/8254, 1981) and later HPET are programmable timers that raise an interrupt at a chosen rate. This is the hardware Node 7 needed.
  • The TSC (Time Stamp Counter) is a register inside the processor that counts cycles, readable with the rdtsc instruction in a couple of nanoseconds without entering the kernel. It is the fastest source by a wide margin and is what all high-resolution timing uses — but it was historically treacherous, because it counted actual cycles and processors change frequency, and each core had its own counter that could disagree with its neighbours. Modern processors provide an invariant TSC that ticks at a constant rate regardless of frequency scaling and is synchronised across cores, which is what made cheap high-resolution timing possible. /sys/devices/system/clocksource/clocksource0/current_clocksource tells you which source your kernel picked; on a virtual machine it may be kvm-clock or xen rather than tsc, and the difference is roughly twenty nanoseconds versus several hundred per reading.

The tick, and why it went away

Classically the kernel programmed a timer to interrupt at a fixed rate called HZ — 100 on early Linux, later configurable at 100, 250, 300 or 1000. Each interrupt is a tick, and the count of ticks since boot is jiffies. On every tick the kernel updated time, charged the running process, and checked whether its slice had expired.

A fixed tick has a real cost that only became obvious when the context changed. At HZ=1000 the machine takes a thousand interrupts per second even when completely idle, which prevents the processor from entering deep low-power states — bad for battery life, and, on a virtualisation host running two hundred idle guest machines, two hundred thousand pointless interrupts a second, all of which the host must handle. So Linux moved to a tickless design (NO_HZ_IDLE, and later NO_HZ_FULL for cores running a single task): rather than a fixed drumbeat, the kernel programs the timer for whenever the next thing is actually due. An idle machine takes essentially no timer interrupts. [AWARE] This is why "the kernel wakes up every 4 ms" is no longer true and why power-management advice from 2008 does not apply.

The clocks a program can read, and choosing wrongly

Linux exposes several clocks through one call, clock_gettime(clockid, &ts). The distinction between the first two is the single most practically important thing in this node.

  • CLOCK_REALTIME — wall-clock time: seconds since 00:00:00 UTC on 1 January 1970, the Unix epoch. It is what you want when you need to know what time it is in the world. It can jump. Forwards or backwards, at any moment, because an administrator set it or because NTP corrected it. Subtracting two readings of it can yield a negative number.
  • CLOCK_MONOTONIC — nanoseconds since some unspecified point (in practice, boot). It only ever increases. It is meaningless as a date and it is the only correct thing to use for measuring a duration. It does not advance while the machine is suspended.
  • CLOCK_BOOTTIME — like monotonic, but does include suspended time. The right one for a laptop-facing timeout.
  • CLOCK_PROCESS_CPUTIME_ID — processor time consumed by this process, not elapsed time. This is what process.cpuUsage() reads.

The rule that follows: never measure an interval with a wall clock. [EXPLAIN] A rate limiter, a timeout, a cache expiry, a retry backoff, or a benchmark that subtracts two wall-clock readings is a bug waiting for an NTP correction. It has happened at scale repeatedly — a clock step of a few seconds turns "has this token expired" into nonsense, and a backwards step makes durations negative, which then flows into code that assumed non-negative.

Keeping the clock right: NTP, slew, step, and leap seconds

Since the crystal drifts, machines synchronise against reference clocks over the network. NTP (Network Time Protocol, David Mills, 1985, and still in use) estimates both offset and round-trip delay by exchanging timestamps, and organises servers into strata — stratum 0 is a physical reference such as an atomic clock or GPS receiver, stratum 1 is a machine directly attached to one, and so on.

Correcting the clock is done in one of two ways, and knowing which your machine does matters. Stepping jumps the clock to the right value — fast, and it makes time discontinuous, which is exactly the hazard above. Slewing changes the clock's rate slightly, running it fast or slow until it catches up — so time stays monotonic and continuous, at the cost of taking a while. Standard practice is to slew small corrections and step large ones; chrony, now the default on most distributions, does this and converges much faster than the original ntpd, which matters on virtual machines whose clocks drift more.

Leap seconds are the ugliest case. The Earth's rotation is not exactly 86,400 SI seconds per day, so occasionally a second is inserted into UTC, and the time 23:59:60 exists. Unix time, by definition, has exactly 86,400 seconds per day, so it cannot represent it, and systems handle it by repeating a second — meaning wall-clock time genuinely goes backwards. On 30 June 2012 a leap second caused a wave of outages: a Linux kernel bug produced a livelock in hrtimer code that spiked CPU to 100% on huge numbers of machines, and Java applications using Thread.sleep hung. Reddit, Mozilla, Qantas's booking system and others went down. The industry's answer since is leap smearing — Google's approach, now widespread, spreading the extra second across a 24-hour window as a tiny rate change, so no discontinuity ever occurs. Machines using smeared NTP disagree with machines using standard NTP by up to a second during the smear, which is its own hazard.

The vDSO: reading the clock without a system call

Reading the time is one of the most frequent requests a program makes — every log line, every metric, every timeout check. At 50 to 100 nanoseconds per system call (Node 4), a program calling it a million times a second spends five to ten percent of a core purely crossing the boundary.

The fix is elegant. The kernel maps a small page of code and data into every process's address space, read-only, called the vDSO (virtual dynamic shared object). It contains a copy of the current time-keeping parameters, updated by the kernel, and a function that reads the TSC and applies them — entirely in user mode. So clock_gettime usually costs about twenty nanoseconds and makes no system call at all. [EXPLAIN] You can see it in any process: it appears as [vdso] in /proc/<pid>/maps. This is also why strace shows no syscall for Date.now() — a fact that confuses people the first time they look. Note that when the clock source is not the TSC (some virtual machines), the vDSO path is unavailable and every time reading becomes a real syscall, which is a genuine and measurable performance difference between instance types.

Where this is in your stack

The three JavaScript time functions, and which to use. Date.now() is CLOCK_REALTIME in milliseconds — correct for "what time is it", wrong for durations. performance.now() is monotonic, sub-millisecond, and measured from a page or process start — correct for durations. process.hrtime.bigint() is monotonic in nanoseconds — correct for durations where you need the resolution. Anything that computes Date.now() - start to enforce a timeout or record a latency is subtly wrong, and the bug appears only when a clock correction lands mid-measurement, which is to say rarely and at the worst time.

performance.now() is deliberately degraded, and the reason is a processor bug. After Spectre (January 2018) high-resolution timers became an attack tool, because a side-channel attack needs to distinguish a cache hit from a miss — a difference of tens of nanoseconds. Browsers responded by coarsening performance.now() to 100 microseconds and adding random jitter; the current position is around 5 microseconds in cross-origin-isolated contexts and coarser otherwise. So the resolution of a browser timer is set by a 2018 hardware vulnerability. Node, not being a hostile-code environment in the same way, keeps full resolution.

setTimeout(fn, 1000) guarantees a minimum, never a maximum, and the reasons stack up. Node's timers are held in lists sorted by expiry, and the loop's epoll_wait is given a timeout equal to the time until the nearest one. So a timer fires only when the loop reaches its timers phase, which means: if a callback is running when the timer expires, the timer waits (the cooperative scheduler from Node 7); if the machine is loaded, the kernel scheduler adds its own delay; and the kernel's own timer granularity adds a little more. A 1000 ms timer under load can fire at 1400 ms and nothing is broken. setTimeout(fn, 0) is not "run immediately" — it is clamped to 1 ms in Node, and in browsers the HTML specification clamps to 4 ms once five nested timeouts have accumulated, a rule that dates from 2010 and exists because sites used setTimeout(f, 0) as a busy loop. If you want "after this callback finishes, before any I/O", the correct tool is setImmediate in Node or a microtask (queueMicrotask, or an already-resolved promise) anywhere — and Node 20 explains exactly where each of those lands.

Background tabs are throttled aggressively, and this breaks polling code. A hidden tab's timers are limited to roughly once per second; Chrome added "intensive throttling" in version 88 (2021) reducing that to once per minute after five minutes hidden. So a dashboard that polls with setInterval stops polling when you switch tabs and produces a burst on return. The correct designs are visibilitychange plus an explicit refetch, a Server-Sent Events or WebSocket push, or a Service Worker — and libraries like TanStack Query have refetchOnWindowFocus precisely because of this platform behaviour.

setInterval drifts and setTimeout chains do not overlap. setInterval(f, 1000) schedules from a fixed base, so if f takes 300 ms the executions land at 1000, 2000, 3000 — but if f is async and takes 1200 ms you get overlapping executions, which for a polling job means two requests in flight and, for a cron-like job, double processing. A self-rescheduling setTimeout at the end of the work cannot overlap, at the cost of drifting by the work's duration each round. Neither is right in general; choose knowing which failure you prefer.

Clock skew breaks authentication, and the errors are unhelpful. A JWT carries iat, nbf and exp as wall-clock seconds. If the issuing server's clock is 30 seconds ahead of the verifying server's, a freshly issued token is not yet valid and verification fails with an error about the token not being active, which reads like a bug in your code. Every JWT library therefore has a clockTolerance option, typically set to 30 or 60 seconds, and this is what it is for. TOTP two-factor codes have the same problem, which is why they accept the adjacent time window, and TLS certificate validation has it too — a machine with a badly wrong clock cannot establish HTTPS connections at all, which is a genuinely confusing failure to debug on an embedded device or a fresh virtual machine.

Postgres distinguishes the clocks explicitly and most people use the wrong one by accident. now() and CURRENT_TIMESTAMP return the time the transaction started and do not change within it — so a thousand rows inserted in one transaction all share a timestamp, which is usually what you want for consistency. clock_timestamp() reads the actual clock each call. statement_timestamp() is per-statement. Similarly timestamptz stores an absolute instant (in UTC) while timestamp stores a wall-clock reading with no zone at all, and storing local times in a timestamp column is one of the most durable sources of data corruption in application development — the ambiguity becomes unrecoverable at a daylight-saving transition, where one local hour occurs twice.

Across machines, timestamps cannot order events, and this is a hard limit rather than an engineering shortfall. Two servers' clocks differ by milliseconds at best. So if server A writes at its 10:00:00.100 and server B writes at its 10:00:00.050, you cannot conclude B happened first. Systems that need ordering therefore do not use wall clocks: they use logical clocks (Lamport timestamps, vector clocks), hybrid logical clocks (CockroachDB), or, in Google Spanner's case, an explicitly bounded uncertainty interval maintained by GPS and atomic clocks in every datacentre — TrueTime returns a range rather than an instant, and the database waits out the uncertainty before committing. That design exists because the alternative is incorrect. For your own systems the practical version is: use database-generated sequences or monotonic IDs (ULID, Snowflake) for ordering, not client timestamps, and never resolve a conflict with "last write wins by wall clock" unless you have accepted that it will sometimes discard the newer write.

What you can do at this point: name the clock sources and pick the right one for a purpose, explain why intervals must never be measured with Date.now(), explain the vDSO and why Date.now() makes no syscall, explain exactly what setTimeout promises and what timer throttling does to polling code, and explain why cross-machine timestamps cannot order events.

What's still broken: the processor is now genuinely shared — many processes, fairly scheduled, preemptible, with an accurate clock. But they all still live in one memory. Every process from Node 5 has an address space in its task_struct, and nothing has yet explained how two processes can both use address 0x400000 for different data, how a program compiled on one machine can be loaded at any address on another, or how one process is prevented from reading another's memory.
