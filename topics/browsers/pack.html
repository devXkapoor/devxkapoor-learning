PART I — THE MACHINE, FROM NOTHING

Node 0 — a wire that is either on or off

Start with a wire. Not a metaphor — a strip of metal with electricity in it or not.

You can push electricity into one end of a wire, and at the other end you can detect whether it's there. That's the whole capability. If you attach a little pressure meter to the far end, you'll read some voltage — a measure of how hard the electricity is being pushed. Call the two situations "about zero volts" and "about one volt."

So the smallest thing this machine can do is distinguish two situations. That's the floor. Everything above it is built out of nothing else.

Now the obvious objection, and it's the right one: why two? A wire can carry any voltage between zero and one. Why not agree that 0.0 volts means the digit 0, 0.1 volts means 1, 0.2 means 2, and so on up to nine? Ten times as much meaning on the same wire. Free.

People genuinely built machines that way. ENIAC, finished in 1945 at the University of Pennsylvania, worked in decimal — it stored each digit as a ring of ten circuits, and exactly one of them was on. So this isn't hindsight cleverness; it was a live option and it lost.

It lost because of noise. A wire is not a sealed pipe. Its voltage sags a little over distance, wobbles when the wire next to it switches, drifts when the room warms up, and dips when something else in the machine draws power. All of that is small — a few hundredths of a volt. Which is fatal if your digits are one tenth of a volt apart, because 0.3 volts sagging to 0.28 is now ambiguous between "three" and "two", and nothing downstream can tell that an error occurred. But if the only two legal values are "near zero" and "near one," then a wobble of 0.05 volts is nothing. Anything below about 0.4 is read as off; anything above about 0.6 is read as on; the middle is a no-man's-land you design your circuits never to sit in. Noise gets thrown away at every single step instead of accumulating.

That is the entire reason computers are binary, and it's an engineering trade, not a mathematical truth: you give up nine tenths of the information a wire could carry, and in exchange every value is either exactly right or catastrophically wrong, never slightly wrong. [EXPLAIN] A machine that is occasionally slightly wrong is worthless. A machine that is exactly right is the thing you can build a civilisation on.

The two states get names. On is called 1, off is called 0, and one wire's worth of on-or-off is called a bit — a contraction of "binary digit," a word Claude Shannon put into print in 1948. A bit is not an abstract unit of information here. It's a wire, and whether there's electricity in it. [EXPLAIN]

One more piece and Node 0 is done. We need a wire whose state can be controlled by another wire, because otherwise nothing can compute anything — a machine that only carries values you put in by hand is a bundle of cables.

That component is the transistor, invented at Bell Labs in December 1947 by John Bardeen, Walter Brattain and William Shockley. Think of it as a tap with three connections: an input, an output, and a control. Put voltage on the control wire and the tap opens, so the input flows to the output. Take the voltage off the control and the tap shuts. A switch that is operated by electricity rather than by a finger. [EXPLAIN]

That's the atom. Everything — the browser you have twelve tabs open in, the encryption on your bank login, this sentence — is that one component, made very small and repeated. A chip in a phone has somewhere north of ten billion of them.

And the reason it's enough is that switches can be wired to make decisions. Put two transistors in a row: electricity only reaches the end if both control wires are on. That's an AND. Put two side by side: the end is on if either is on. That's an OR. Wire one so that the output is on when the control is off — a NOT. Those three, combined, can compute any function of bits that can be defined at all. Adding two numbers is a specific arrangement of a few dozen of them. There's no additional magic ingredient introduced later. It is switches, all the way up.

What you can do at this point: hold one piece of information — a yes or a no — in a wire, and build gadgets that decide things from a few of them.

What's still broken: one wire holds one bit, and one bit can say almost nothing. You cannot count to three with it. And the instant you stop pushing electricity, it forgets.

Node 1 — counting with switches

One wire, two possible states. So put wires side by side and read them together as a group. Two wires have four combinations: off-off, off-on, on-off, on-on. Three wires have eight. Each wire you add doubles the number of combinations, because every pattern the old wires could make now exists twice — once with the new wire off, once with it on. [EXPLAIN]

Doubling gets big fast: 8 wires give 256 combinations, 16 give 65,536, 32 give about 4.3 billion.

To use those combinations as numbers you need one agreed rule for which pattern means which number, and the natural rule is the one you already use in decimal, with 2 in place of 10. In decimal, the digits of 307 mean three hundreds, no tens, seven ones — each place worth ten times the one to its right. In binary each place is worth two times the one to its right: 1, 2, 4, 8, 16, 32, 64, 128. So the pattern 01000001 means 0 + 64 + 0 + 0 + 0 + 0 + 0 + 1 = 65. [EXPLAIN] That's all binary is. Same counting you've always done, with a different multiplier per column, because a column here is a wire and a wire has two states.

How many wires should a group be? There's no law. It was a choice, made repeatedly, and it settled. Eight won.

The word byte was coined in 1956 by Werner Buchholz at IBM, working on a machine called Stretch, for "the group of bits you handle as one unit when dealing with a character of text." It bounced around — some machines used six, some seven, some nine — and the thing that froze it at eight was the IBM System/360 in 1964, which was enormously successful and made 8 the size everyone else matched so their tapes and files would interchange. Eight is convenient (it's a power of two, so a byte splits neatly in halves) and it was big enough for the character sets of the day with room to spare. But there is no deeper reason. A byte is 8 bits because IBM shipped a popular machine in 1964. [AWARE]

So: a byte is eight wires, holding a number from 0 to 255. That range is not arbitrary — eight wires make 256 patterns, and if you start counting at zero the largest is 255. Any time you see 255 or 256 anywhere in computing, that's where it came from. Same for 65,535 (sixteen wires) and 4,294,967,295 (thirty-two wires, which is why old machines couldn't use more than about 4 gigabytes of memory — more on that in Node 2). [EXPLAIN]

Now, a caution that matters more than it looks, and that Node 4 is entirely about.

A byte holds a number from 0 to 255. That is all it holds. It does not hold a letter. It does not hold a colour. It does not hold "true." Those are all things that happen in a reader's head, or in another circuit's wiring — never in the byte. The byte is 65 and stays 65.

What you can do at this point: hold a number between 0 and 255 in eight wires, and hold bigger numbers by using more wires.

What's still broken: the moment the power goes, the wires go flat and every number is gone. And more urgently: you have a handful of numbers held in a handful of wires. A machine needs billions of numbers. You cannot run a billion separate wires to a billion separate places and keep track of which is which.

Node 2 — a switch that remembers, and a row of them with numbers on the front

Two problems: forgetting, and quantity. They get solved together, and the solution is the single most important structure in this entire document, so I'm going to be slow about it.

Remembering first. You can make a circle out of switches. Wire two NOT gates — the "output is on when the input is off" gadget from Node 0 — in a loop, so each one's output feeds the other's input. Now the pair has exactly two stable arrangements, and it will sit in whichever one it was put into, indefinitely, as long as power is supplied. Nudge it with a third wire and it flips to the other arrangement and stays there. That's a flip-flop: one bit that holds its value. [EXPLAIN] It's fast — it settles in well under a nanosecond — and it costs about six transistors per bit, which is a lot when you want billions.

So for bulk storage, machines use something cheaper. In 1966 Robert Dennard at IBM worked out that you could store a bit as a tiny bit of electric charge held on a microscopic plate, guarded by a single transistor: charge present means 1, no charge means 0. One transistor and one plate per bit instead of six transistors. That's DRAM, and it's what the sticks of RAM in your machine are made of. The catch is right there in the name — dynamic — because the charge leaks away in a few milliseconds. So the memory controller walks the entire chip continuously, reading every location and writing it straight back to top it up, roughly every 64 milliseconds, forever, while the power is on. Your RAM is not sitting still; it is being frantically re-remembered thousands of times a second. [AWARE] And it is entirely dependent on power. Cut it, and every charge drains, and everything is gone within seconds. That's why RAM is "volatile," and it's why a crash loses your unsaved work — not policy, physics.

Now quantity, which is the beautiful part.

You have a billion of these one-bit cells on a chip. You need a way to say "I want that one" without a billion wires.

The trick: give every cell a number, and send the number down a small bundle of wires. A circuit inside the chip — a decoder — takes that number and activates exactly one of the billion cells. And a number, from Node 1, is just a pattern on a few wires. Thirty-two wires can express 4.3 billion different numbers. So thirty-two wires can select any one of 4.3 billion locations. You've replaced a billion wires with thirty-two.

That number is called an address, and it is not a thing stored anywhere. It is which cell you are pointing at. The bundle of wires that carries it is the address bus; the separate bundle that carries the byte in or out is the data bus. [EXPLAIN]

Cells are grouped so that one address selects a whole byte — eight cells — rather than one bit, because addressing individual bits would triple the size of every address for very little benefit. So:

Memory is a very long row of numbered boxes. Each box holds one byte, a number from 0 to 255. The number on the front of the box is its address. [EXPLAIN]

Hold onto that sentence. It is going to explain the DOM, closures, promises, and every memory leak you ever have.

The row is long. A machine with 8 gigabytes has about 8.6 billion boxes, addressed 0 through 8,589,934,591. And now the "4 gigabyte limit" of old machines is not trivia, it's arithmetic: with 32 wires you can name 4,294,967,296 distinct boxes and not one more, so no matter how much memory you plug in, there is no address that reaches it. Sixty-four wires can name about 18 quintillion, which is more boxes than anyone will build, which is why the limit stopped mattering. [EXPLAIN]

Two operations exist. Read: put an address on the address bus, wait, take the byte off the data bus. Write: put an address on the address bus and a byte on the data bus, pulse the write wire.

That's the whole interface to memory. Two operations, and boxes with numbers.

And already the shape of a hundred future problems is visible. Boxes have no idea what's in them. Box 4096 does not know it's part of a picture; it holds 65 and would hold 65 just as happily if it were part of your name. Nothing stops you reading a box you never wrote — you'll get whatever was there, which is whatever the last program left. Nothing stops you writing to a box that belongs to something else and destroying it. All of Node 6's protection machinery exists because the row of boxes offers none.

What you can do at this point: store and retrieve billions of numbers, and get any one of them back by naming it.

What's still broken: nothing is happening. This is a warehouse. Something has to decide which boxes to read, what to compute, and where to put the answer — and right now that something is you, by hand, one operation at a time.

Node 3 — a machine that reads its own instructions

You could build a fixed circuit that does one specific job — the 1940s did exactly this, and reprogramming ENIAC meant physically replugging cables for days.

The idea that ends that, written down by John von Neumann in a 1945 draft describing a machine called EDVAC and first actually run on a machine in Manchester in June 1948, is startling in its simplicity:

Put the instructions in the memory, as numbers, alongside the data.

That's it. That's the stored-program computer, and it is why software exists as a separate thing from hardware. Instructions are numbers; numbers live in boxes; so instructions live in boxes, and a different set of numbers in those boxes makes the machine do something else entirely, with no rewiring.

Here's the machine that consumes them.

The CPU has a small number of its own internal one-byte-or-eight-byte holders, built from the fast flip-flops of Node 2 rather than the slow leaky DRAM. These are registers — call it sixteen of them on a typical chip. They're scratch space, right next to the arithmetic circuits, readable in a fraction of a nanosecond. Everything the CPU actually computes with must be in a register; memory is where things are kept, registers are where things are worked on. [EXPLAIN]

One register is special. It holds the address of the next instruction to execute. It's called the program counter, or on Intel chips the instruction pointer. [EXPLAIN]

And now the entire behaviour of every computer that has ever existed:

forever:
    read the byte(s) at the address in the instruction pointer
    work out what instruction that number means
    do it
    move the instruction pointer past it

Fetch, decode, execute, repeat. Nothing else. Ever. [EXPLAIN]

The instructions are tiny and there aren't many kinds that matter:

  • Copy a byte from a memory address into a register. (mov)
  • Copy a register back out to a memory address.
  • Add two registers. Subtract. Multiply. Bitwise and/or/not.
  • Compare two registers, which just does a subtraction and throws the answer away, keeping only a couple of flag bits recording whether the result was zero or negative.
  • Set the instruction pointer to a given address instead of just advancing it — a jump.
  • Jump only if a flag bit is set — a conditional jump.

That last pair is worth stopping on, because it is every piece of control flow you have ever written. An if is a compare followed by a conditional jump over the block. A while loop is the body, then a compare, then a conditional jump backwards to the start. A function call is a jump to its address. There is no separate machinery for loops or branches. There is only "sometimes, put a different number in the instruction pointer." [EXPLAIN]

The numbers are real. On an Intel-style chip, the single byte 0xC3 — 195 in decimal — means "return from the current function." A byte holding 195 in the middle of a photograph is part of a shade of grey; the same byte at the address the instruction pointer is looking at makes the machine jump somewhere else. The byte is identical. The difference is entirely which register was pointing at it. [EXPLAIN]

Speed, so you have real numbers for later. The whole thing is driven by a clock — a wire pulsing on and off billions of times a second, keeping every circuit stepping in time. At 3 gigahertz that's 3 billion pulses per second, so one pulse is about a third of a nanosecond, and simple instructions take roughly one pulse. Hold that: a CPU does something like three billion small things every second. Node 8 is going to put that next to how long a network request takes, and the comparison is the reason this entire document exists.

What you can do at this point: run a program. The machine reads its own instructions out of memory, computes, decides, loops, and writes results back — with nobody touching it.

What's still broken: we've been sloppy in a way that's about to cause real trouble. I said "a byte holding 195 is part of a photograph," as if a photograph were a thing memory could contain. It isn't. There are only numbers in boxes. Where does meaning come from?

Node 4 — bytes mean nothing; meaning is a convention laid on top

This node adds no hardware. It's a shift in how you see what's already there, and I'd argue it's the single most useful idea in the whole of Part I — because almost every "wait, how does that even work" moment in the browser dissolves the moment you're properly holding it.

The row of boxes contains numbers. Only numbers. There is no type, no tag, no label, no hint. Box 5000 holds 72. It does not hold "the letter H." It does not hold "72 percent." It holds 72.

Meaning is entirely, always, a convention agreed on by whatever code reads it. [EXPLAIN] And the conventions are all inventions with dates and authors.

Text. To write text you must first agree which number stands for which letter, and that agreement is a table someone wrote down. ASCII, published in 1963, said: 65 is A, 66 is B, 97 is a, 32 is a space, 48 is the character 0. Seven bits, 128 slots, which was plenty for American English and hopeless for everyone else — so the eighth bit got used for a second hundred-odd characters, and every country used it differently, and a file written in Warsaw opened as gibberish in Lisbon. That mess is why Unicode exists: one table for every writing system, now covering about 150,000 characters. And because most of those need more than one byte, there's a further convention for how to spread a character across several bytes: UTF-8, sketched by Ken Thompson and Rob Pike in September 1992, which is cleverly built so that the original ASCII numbers still mean exactly what they always did, and only characters beyond 127 spill into extra bytes. [USE]

But re-read that paragraph and notice what never happened: no byte anywhere became a letter. A text file is boxes of numbers, and a program that has been told "treat these as UTF-8" draws letter-shapes accordingly. Point a different program at the same boxes with a different convention and you get different letters, or noise. The famous garbled â€™ in the middle of a web page is exactly this: bytes written under one convention, read under another. Nothing is corrupt. The numbers are perfect. The agreement broke. [EXPLAIN]

Pictures. Same story. Agree that each pixel is four consecutive boxes — how much red, how much green, how much blue, how opaque — each 0 to 255. Then a 1920×1080 image is 1920 × 1080 × 4 = 8,294,400 boxes, and "the image" is that run of boxes plus the agreement about how to read them.

Bigger numbers. A box holds up to 255, so to hold 100,000 you use four consecutive boxes and agree to treat them as one 32-bit number. Which immediately raises a question with no correct answer: does the first box hold the biggest part or the smallest? Both were chosen by different manufacturers, they're called big-endian and little-endian, and the names come from Gulliver's Travels — a satire about a war over which end of a boiled egg to crack — because the engineer who named them thought the dispute was equally silly. It's still with us, and it's why network protocols have to specify a byte order. [AWARE]

True and false. Agree that 0 is false and anything else is true. There is no boolean in the boxes.

Nothing. And here's the one that explains a daily annoyance. You need a way to say "this doesn't point anywhere." So you pick a number that will never be a valid address and use it as the marker. Zero was the obvious pick. Now every piece of code that follows a stored address must check for zero first — and the ones that forget follow it, and the machine tries to read box zero, and everything stops. Tony Hoare introduced this in 1965 and in 2009 called it his "billion-dollar mistake." Every Cannot read properties of null, every undefined is not a function, every null-pointer crash in every language, is a descendant of that choice. [EXPLAIN]

So the honest picture of memory: an enormous row of numbered boxes containing numbers, over which many different agreements are laid, none of which are visible in the boxes themselves. Program code, text, images, addresses of other boxes — all the same substance. The only thing that distinguishes them is which code is looking, and what it has assumed.

What you can do at this point: represent anything at all — text, images, structures, other addresses — as numbers in boxes, and know that "what type is this" is never a property of memory but always a property of the code reading it.

What's still broken: we can run a program, but only as one long unbroken sequence. There's no way to write "do this useful thing" once and use it from three places — and worse, no way to do it at all, because as soon as one part of a program calls another, a question appears that we have no machinery to answer: where does the called part keep its own variables, and how does it know how to get back?

Node 5 — the function call, and the stack it needs

This is the last piece of pure machine, and the one everything from await to a stack trace to a closure is eventually made of.

The problem stated exactly. Say a piece of code does a job — it has a local variable, total, and works with it. That value has to live in some box. Which one?

The tempting answer is: decide when the program is written. Give total box 5000, permanently.

That works right up until this code is used from two places at once. It calls something else, which — directly or after four more calls — calls back into it. Now two runs of the same code are live simultaneously, each with its own total, and they both want box 5000. The inner one writes 3. The outer one, when it eventually resumes, reads box 5000 expecting its own value and finds 3. Silent, total corruption.

So the boxes for a call's variables cannot be chosen in advance. They have to be handed out when the call starts and taken back when it ends. And there's a second, sharper problem: from Node 3, a call is a jump — you set the instruction pointer to the code's address. But then how does it get back? The code being called might be invoked from a hundred places; it can't have one hard-coded return address.

Both problems have the same solution, and it's almost embarrassingly simple.

Set aside a region of memory for this purpose. Add one more register — the stack pointer — holding the address of the boundary between "currently in use" and "free" within that region. Then agree on a discipline, and this is the whole of it: [EXPLAIN]

On a call:

  1. Write the address of the instruction after the call into a box just past the boundary. That's the return address.
  2. Move the stack pointer along, past enough boxes for the called code's local variables. Those boxes are now that call's private scratch space.
  3. Set the instruction pointer to the called code's address.

On a return:

  1. Read the return address out of the block.
  2. Move the stack pointer back to where it was.
  3. Set the instruction pointer to the return address.

That block — a return address plus that call's locals — is a stack frame. [EXPLAIN] And the region is called a stack because frames pile up as calls go deeper and come off in exactly reverse order: if render calls format which calls pad, there are three frames, and pad's must come off before format's, because format's locals are underneath. That ordering isn't a rule anyone imposed. It falls straight out of using one moving boundary over one region.

Now the two problems are gone, and gone for the same reason. Two live runs of the same code sit at different depths, so their frames are in different places, so their totals are different boxes. And each frame carries its own return address, so the same code can be called from a hundred places and get home from all of them.

Three things you already recognise are now fully explained, and they're not analogies — they're descriptions of memory:

A stack trace is that pile printed from the top down. Each line is one frame, and each frame is there because it's waiting for the one above it to finish. That's why the trace shows you the path rather than just a location.

Stack overflow — the region has a fixed size, chosen when the program starts, typically 8 megabytes on Linux and 1 megabyte on Windows, which is "big enough for any sane call depth." Recursion with no base case pushes frame after frame until the boundary walks off the end of the region. The machine catches it and kills the program, because the alternative is trampling whatever memory sits beyond. [EXPLAIN]

Returning doesn't erase anything. Step 2 of a return just moves a pointer. The old frame's bytes sit there untouched until the next call moves the boundary forward and writes over them. Which is why, in languages that let you look, a "freed" variable often still seems to hold its value for a while — you're reading a box nobody has gotten around to reusing yet.

And now the sentence that Part IV will spend four nodes on, planted here where it costs nothing:

A running function's identity is its stack frame. When the function returns, the frame is abandoned — the boundary moves back and those boxes become available. There is no mechanism, anywhere in what we have built, for a function to stop halfway, keep its frame, let something else run, and come back to it.

The stack goes one way. That is the whole point of it, and it's what makes it fast and simple. When you reach await in Node 31 and it appears to do precisely the impossible thing — pause in the middle, let the entire browser carry on, and resume later with all its variables intact — you'll know exactly which law is being broken and will want to know how. The answer is that it isn't broken; the function is quietly taken apart and stored somewhere that isn't the stack. But that's Node 30.

What you can do at this point: write real programs with functions that call functions, each with its own variables, each able to return to whoever called it.

What's still broken: one program owns the entire machine. It can read and write any box, including the boxes holding another program's data or its own instructions. Two programs cannot run at once — nothing decides who gets the chip. A program that loops forever locks the machine and only the power switch ends it. And this program cannot talk to a disk, a network, a keyboard or a screen, because nothing here knows those exist.

Next: Node 6 — one machine, many programs, where the operating system arrives, and with it privilege, the system call, the timer interrupt that lets anything be taken off the chip against its will, and the private memory world that will eventually be the reason your bank tab cannot be read by the tab next to it. Then Node 7 builds the thread on top of it.

Node 6 — one machine, many programs

The limitation: the program we built in Node 5 owns everything. It can read or write any box in the row, it holds the chip until it stops, and it has no way to reach a disk, a keyboard or a network, because nothing in Nodes 0–5 knows those exist.

Every one of those is a separate problem, and they got solved by one idea applied four times: put another program underneath, and take some abilities away from everyone else.

First, the thing that makes "underneath" possible

If the underneath-program is just another program made of the same instructions, what stops the top program from doing whatever the underneath one can do? Nothing — unless the chip itself distinguishes them. So chips grew a distinction, and it's small: one extra bit of state inside the CPU, saying which mode it's currently running in.

  • In kernel mode (Intel calls it ring 0), every instruction is allowed.
  • In user mode (ring 3), a handful of instructions are forbidden. Attempt one and the chip refuses and jumps elsewhere instead — which we'll get to in a moment.

The forbidden ones are exactly the ones that would let you escape: instructions that talk directly to hardware, instructions that change the memory-translation tables from later in this node, and the instruction that changes the mode bit itself. [EXPLAIN]

The program that runs in kernel mode is the kernel. It's loaded first, when the machine powers on, and it sets everything else up. Everything you ever run — browser, editor, game — runs in user mode, with those instructions unavailable. The operating system is the kernel plus the pile of ordinary programs shipped alongside it. [EXPLAIN]

Second: how anything gets taken off the chip against its will

From Node 3, the chip does one thing forever: read the instruction at the instruction pointer, do it, advance. If a program loops forever, that loop is what the chip does forever. Nothing in Nodes 0–5 can stop it, because "stopping it" would mean somebody else's instructions running, and the pointer is inside the loop.

So chips gained a wire. Physically: a pin, which some other piece of hardware can put voltage on. When it goes high, the chip finishes the instruction it's on and then, without asking anyone, does this:

  1. Saves the current instruction pointer somewhere safe.
  2. Switches the mode bit to kernel mode.
  3. Sets the instruction pointer to a fixed address that was written into a table when the machine booted.

That's an interrupt. [EXPLAIN] The running program is not consulted, is not notified, and cannot decline. One instruction it's executing its own code; the next it isn't, and the kernel is. The table of "which address to jump to for which interrupt" is set up once at boot, by the kernel, using an instruction that user mode isn't allowed to execute — which is why a program can't redirect interrupts to itself.

Now put a timer chip on that wire, configured to pulse every few milliseconds. Every few milliseconds, no matter what any program is doing, the kernel gets the chip back. That's the foundation of everything else in this node. [EXPLAIN]

Worth knowing that this wasn't always so, because the alternative is the exact shape of the browser's main thread and you'll recognise it immediately. Early Windows (up to 3.1) and the classic Mac OS used cooperative multitasking: no timer interrupt was used to force a switch, and instead each program was expected to voluntarily call a system function every so often to say "I'm at a good stopping point, let someone else run." It worked when programs behaved. And when one program hit a long loop or a bug and simply never called that function, the entire machine froze — mouse included — and you power-cycled it. That's why Windows 95 and NT moved to preemptive multitasking, where the timer takes control whether you like it or not. [AWARE]

Hold that comparison. In Node 19, you'll find that JavaScript in a browser is cooperative, deliberately, for reasons as good as Netscape's reasons in 1995 — and that every frozen tab you've ever seen is the 1993 Mac failure mode, alive and well, on purpose.

Third: how a program reaches a disk or a network

A program in user mode can't touch hardware. But it obviously needs to. So there's a controlled doorway: one special instruction (syscall on modern Intel chips) whose entire job is "switch to kernel mode and jump to the kernel's entry point," with a number in a register saying what's wanted — read from a file, send bytes on a network connection, allocate more memory.

That is a system call. [EXPLAIN] It's the only way out of user mode, it lands on an address the kernel chose, and the kernel checks whether you're allowed to do what you asked before doing it. Every file your browser reads, every packet it sends, every megabyte of memory it gets, goes through this doorway. There is no other route.

And it's worth noticing what a system call costs: switching modes, saving registers, entering the kernel and coming back is on the order of hundreds of nanoseconds to a microsecond — hundreds or thousands of instructions' worth. Cheap compared to a disk, ruinous if you do it per byte. Which is why almost everything is buffered: reading a file 4096 bytes at a time instead of one at a time is not a style preference, it's avoiding a thousand trips through that doorway.

Fourth, and biggest: giving each program its own row of boxes

Node 2 left us with one row of numbered boxes, and Node 4 established that a box is just a number with no owner and no label. So your browser could read box 500,000 and find your password manager's data, or write to it and destroy it. Not maliciously — a single wrong address in a loop does it.

The fix is to make it impossible to name another program's memory, and this is the most elegant thing in the whole node.

The addresses programs use are not real. When your code says "read the address 4096," that number goes to a piece of hardware called the memory management unit, which looks it up in a table — a table the kernel built for this program specifically, and which the program has no permission to modify — and translates it into some entirely different physical box number. Your 4096 might be physical box 8,431,616. Another program's 4096 is translated by its table into a completely different physical box.

The number the program uses is a virtual address. The one that goes out on the wires from Node 2 is a physical address. Programs only ever see virtual ones. [EXPLAIN]

Doing this per byte would need a translation table with billions of entries, so memory is translated in chunks called pages, almost universally 4096 bytes each. One table entry covers a whole page; the low 12 bits of the address just pick the byte within it, untranslated. Why 4096? It's a power of two, and it's the middle of a trade: bigger pages mean a smaller table but more waste when you need only a few bytes; smaller pages mean less waste but a table too big to hold. 4 kilobytes was settled on decades ago and stuck. [AWARE]

Now the payoff. If a virtual address has no entry in your table, there is no physical box it can reach. Not "access denied" — unreachable, there is no such place from where you're standing. The hardware raises an interrupt, the kernel takes over, and typically kills your program. That is a segmentation fault. [EXPLAIN] And other programs' memory has no entries in your table at all. You cannot touch it because you cannot name it.

This buys other things almost for free, which is why it's universal:

  • Every program can believe it starts at address 0 and has the whole range to itself, so programs don't need to be compiled for wherever they happen to land.
  • A page can be marked read-only, so a program's own instructions can't be overwritten by a stray write; or no-execute, so data can never be run as instructions — a defence against a whole class of attacks.
  • A page can be marked "not currently in RAM." Touch it and the interrupt fires, the kernel fetches the page from disk into some free physical box, updates the table, and restarts your instruction as if nothing happened. That's swapping, and it's why a machine under memory pressure gets slow rather than crashing.
  • Two programs' tables can point at the same physical page, which is how a shared library exists once in RAM but appears in fifty programs, and how two programs deliberately share memory when they want to.

And so: the process

Everything above assembles into one container.

A process is a running program together with its own private mapping of addresses to physical memory, its own permissions, and its own list of open files and connections. [EXPLAIN]

A process is a world. Two processes are two worlds with no shared vocabulary of addresses. The kernel maintains a record for each one, keeps a list of them, and when it wants to switch from one to another it loads that process's translation table into the MMU — after which the same virtual address means something completely different, and the previous world is simply unreachable.

That is the isolation your machine runs on. And in Node 39 you'll find that a browser deliberately puts different websites in different processes for exactly this reason: not because it trusts its own code to check carefully, but so that a hostile page has no address it could even try.

What you can do at this point: run many programs at once on one machine, none able to touch another's memory, none able to hold the chip forever, all able to reach disks and networks through a controlled doorway.

What's still broken: a process has one instruction pointer — one position in its own code. So while it's waiting for a disk, it's doing nothing at all. And on a machine with eight cores, one process can only ever use one of them. There's no way for a single program to be in two places.

Node 7 — a thread is a position, and a process is a world

The limitation: one process, one position. Waiting stops everything; extra cores go unused.

We already built everything needed for the answer. From Node 5: a running function's identity is its stack frame, and the pile of frames — the stack — is what records how it got where it is. From Node 3: the instruction pointer says where it is right now. From Node 6: the kernel can save and restore registers at will.

So:

A thread is one instruction pointer plus one stack. [EXPLAIN]

A thread is a position in a program, plus the pile of half-finished calls that got it there. Nothing more. It's a bookmark with a history. And that means a second thread is not conceptually exotic — it's a second stack region somewhere else in the same process's memory, and a second saved set of registers.

Note the containment carefully, because this is the distinction people fumble:

  • A process is a world — its own translation table, so its own private meaning for every address.
  • A thread is a position inside a world. Every thread in one process shares the same translation table, which means the same addresses reach the same physical boxes for all of them.

One process always has at least one thread. It can have hundreds. [EXPLAIN]

How several threads run on one chip

One core has one physical instruction pointer, and Node 3 was not negotiable about that. So two threads on one core never run at the same instant. They take turns, using the machinery of Node 6.

The timer interrupt fires. The chip jumps to kernel code, in kernel mode, whether the running thread liked it or not. The kernel then:

  1. Copies every current register value — instruction pointer, stack pointer, all sixteen scratch registers — into thread A's record in memory.
  2. Picks another thread, B, from its list of runnable ones.
  3. Copies B's saved register values out of B's record and into the actual registers.

The instant it loads B's instruction pointer, the chip is executing B's code at the exact instruction B was interrupted at, with B's stack pointer pointing at B's stack, and every local variable exactly where B left it — because they were never anywhere but in B's own frames in memory. B experiences nothing. It has no way to detect that eleven milliseconds elapsed. [EXPLAIN]

That swap is a context switch, and "context" means literally the saved register values. It costs on the order of one to a few microseconds — a few thousand instructions — which is cheap enough to do a thousand times a second and expensive enough that doing it a million times a second would be all you did. Switching between threads of the same process is cheaper than between processes, because the translation table doesn't have to change.

Now a distinction worth nailing down, because these two words get used as synonyms and are not:

Concurrency is several things being in progress at once — several threads started, none finished — even though the chip executes one at a time. It's about structure.

Parallelism is several things executing in the same instant, which needs more than one core. A laptop with eight cores really is at eight positions simultaneously, and the kernel spreads far more threads than eight across them.

Everything about JavaScript's event loop, which arrives in Node 19, is concurrency. Not parallelism.

The price of sharing a world

Threads in one process share all memory. That's the point — it's what makes them cheap to talk between, compared to processes which share nothing. It's also a loaded gun, and I want to show you the gun firing rather than assert that it might.

Take the most boring line imaginable: count = count + 1. In actual instructions from Node 3, that is three separate steps, because the chip only does tiny things:

  1. Copy the value at count's address into a register.
  2. Add one to the register.
  3. Copy the register back to count's address.

Two threads run that on the same address. count is 5.

Thread A does step 1: its register now holds 5. The timer interrupt fires here — and remember, it can land between any two instructions; it does not ask, and nothing marks these three steps as belonging together. The kernel saves A's registers, including that 5, and switches to B.

Thread B runs all three steps: reads 5, adds one, writes 6. Then it's switched out.

A resumes at step 2, still holding 5 in its restored register. Adds one. Writes 6.

Two increments happened. count is 6. One of them is simply gone, with no error, no crash, nothing to see. Run it a million times and it'll be right most of the time and wrong sometimes, depending on where interrupts happen to land, which depends on what else the machine is doing. That's a race condition [EXPLAIN], and it is the reason multithreaded programming has the reputation it has: the bug appears once a week in production and never on your machine.

Now scale it to a structure. Removing an item from a linked list — a chain where each block of boxes holds the address of the next — means updating the previous block's "next" address and the following block's "previous" address: two separate writes. If another thread walks the chain in the gap between them, it follows an address to a block that has been half-detached, or to memory that has already been handed back and now contains something else entirely. It doesn't get a wrong answer; it reads garbage as if it were addresses and follows it somewhere arbitrary. [EXPLAIN]

Keep that image. In Node 14 the browser builds exactly such a structure of blocks-pointing-at-blocks — the document tree — and this paragraph is the reason only one thread is ever allowed to touch it.

The standard defence is a lock (or mutex, from "mutual exclusion"), and it too is only cells: one byte meaning taken-or-free, plus a special CPU instruction that reads it and sets it in one indivisible step that no interrupt can land inside — the chip guarantees it. A thread that finds it taken asks the kernel to park it until it's free. [USE]

Locks work, and they bring their own problems. Two threads each holding the lock the other needs, both parked forever: a deadlock. Locks taken too broadly, so threads spend their time queueing and the extra cores buy nothing. And the whole thing is a skill — a real one, that people get wrong professionally.

What "blocked" actually means, precisely

Node 6 built the doorway; here's what happens when a thread walks through it and the answer isn't ready.

A thread wants bytes from the network. It executes a system call. The kernel starts the operation and then — since the data won't arrive for a very long time by chip standards — marks that thread not runnable and puts a reference to it on a waiting list attached to that connection. The scheduler now simply skips it: when the timer fires and it's choosing who runs next, that thread isn't a candidate.

So a blocked thread burns no CPU at all. It isn't spinning or checking. It's parked, its saved registers and its stack sitting untouched in memory. When the data eventually arrives, the network hardware raises an interrupt, the kernel copies the bytes in, moves the thread back to the runnable list, and next time round it gets loaded into the registers and continues at the instruction after the system call, having experienced nothing at all.

Two consequences, and both are load-bearing for the rest of this series:

Blocking costs no CPU but costs a thread. A parked thread is unavailable for anything else, and it holds a stack region — on Linux, typically 8 megabytes of address space reserved per thread. If you have one thread and you park it, you have zero threads and the program is stopped. That, exactly, is what will go wrong in Node 21.

Somebody still has to do the waiting. The bytes must be received by some thread. Which is the whole reason the next paragraph exists.

Thread pools

Creating a thread isn't free: the kernel allocates a stack region, builds a record, adds it to its lists — tens of microseconds, plus the memory. Fine once; wasteful if you do it per request on a page making two hundred requests.

So the common pattern: create a fixed batch of threads once at startup, and have each sit in a small loop — look at a shared list of pending jobs; if there's one, take it and do it; if the list is empty, park. Work is handed over by appending to that list (under a lock, because of the increment problem above) and waking a sleeping thread.

That's a thread pool. [EXPLAIN] The size is usually picked from the core count or just fixed at a small number chosen by someone as reasonable — Node.js's default pool is four, adjustable by an environment variable. There's no theory in the number.

The reason this matters here rather than in a systems course: in Node 21, when a web page asks for data and the page doesn't freeze, this is where the waiting goes. Your code hands over a request and returns immediately; a pool thread somewhere else parks in a kernel wait list on your behalf, costing nothing; and when the answer arrives, it doesn't call your code — it can't, for the shared-structure reason above — it appends to a list your thread will read when it's free.

That handoff, list-appending rather than calling, is the mechanical seed of every callback, every promise, and every await in Part IV.

What you can do at this point: have one program be in several positions at once — waiting in one, computing in another, using several cores — and say precisely why that's dangerous and what it costs.

What's still broken: all of Part I so far is inward-facing. This machine still cannot show a human anything, and cannot reach another machine. And a claim has been made repeatedly without evidence — that the outside world is "unimaginably slow." That number needs to be put on the table, because it is the reason the entire second half of this series exists.

Node 8 — everything outside the chip is unimaginably slow

The limitation: Node 7 said blocking "costs a thread" and that waiting takes "a very long time by chip standards," and left both as adjectives. They need to be numbers, because a single ratio is about to become the reason for every strange thing in Parts III and IV.

From Node 3: the clock pulses about three billion times a second, and simple instructions take roughly one pulse. So one instruction takes about a third of a nanosecond — a third of a billionth of a second.

Here's what everything else costs, on the same scale.

WhatHow longIn instructions
One instruction0.33 ns1
Read a byte from the CPU's nearest cache~1 ns~3
Read a byte from main memory (the row of boxes)~80 ns~240
Read a block from an SSD~100,000 ns~300,000
Read a block from a spinning hard disk~10,000,000 ns~30,000,000
Ask a server across the world and get an answer~200,000,000 ns~600,000,000

That last row is the one. In the time it takes to ask a machine in another country a question, this chip could have executed roughly six hundred million instructions.

Multiply everything by three billion to put it in human time, where one instruction becomes one second:

  • reading from cache: 3 seconds
  • reading from memory: 4 minutes
  • reading from an SSD: three and a half days
  • one round trip across the world: nineteen years

Nineteen years. That's the thing your code is "waiting" for. And the machine, during it, is a person sitting perfectly still, doing nothing, for two decades.

Once you're actually holding that, the whole of Part IV stops being a pile of arbitrary syntax and becomes one question asked over and over by different people across twenty-five years: the chip is fast and the world is slow, so what should it be doing during the nineteen years? That's it. That's the entire subject. Callbacks, promises, async, await, the event loop — all of it is answers to that one question, and the answers got progressively less awful.

Why the outside is slow — three unrelated reasons

Distance and the speed of light. Signals in fibre travel at about 200,000 kilometres per second — two thirds of light in vacuum, because glass slows it. Noida to a data centre in Virginia is roughly 12,000 km, so 60 milliseconds one way, 120 there and back, minimum, forever. No engineering fixes this; it's the universe. Real round trips are worse — 200 ms or so — because the path isn't straight and every router along it takes time to look at the packet and forward it. This is why "put the server closer to the user" is a real architectural strategy rather than a micro-optimisation, and it's why Node 10's handshake, which costs one extra round trip before a single useful byte moves, is something people have spent decades trying to delete.

Moving parts. A spinning hard disk physically swings an arm to a track and waits for the platter to rotate the right sector under it — a rotation at 7200 rpm takes 8.3 milliseconds, so on average you wait half of that just for the disk to come round. That's mechanics, in a world of electronics. SSDs have no moving parts and are a hundred times faster, and are still three hundred thousand instructions away.

Distance on the chip, too. Main memory is a separate component, centimetres away across a circuit board, and it's DRAM — the leaky-charge storage from Node 2, which needs time to sense a tiny charge and amplify it. Eighty nanoseconds is mostly waiting for physics, and it has barely improved in twenty years while chips got many times faster. So the gap between the chip and its own memory has been widening.

The fix for one of them, which explains a lot of later behaviour

The memory gap got patched with cache: small amounts of the fast flip-flop storage from Node 2, physically on the CPU, holding recently-used data. Typically three levels — a few tens of kilobytes at ~1 ns, a megabyte or so at ~4 ns, tens of megabytes at ~15 ns — backed by the slow gigabytes of DRAM.

It works because of an empirical fact rather than a law: real programs touch the same boxes repeatedly and touch neighbouring boxes soon after. So the chip never fetches one byte; it fetches a cache line, usually 64 consecutive bytes, betting you'll want the neighbours. [EXPLAIN]

Which is why walking an array in order can be ten times faster than jumping around it randomly, despite being the same number of operations. In order, one fetch serves the next sixteen four-byte items. Randomly, every access is 80 ns of nothing. Nothing in your source code shows this difference. It's the reason a lot of performance advice sounds like superstition until you know about the 64 bytes.

And when the data isn't cached, the chip stalls — literally sits there, unable to proceed, for 240 instructions' worth of time, because the value it needs hasn't arrived. Real chips work around this with tricks (running later independent instructions early, guessing which way a branch will go), and in Node 36 you'll find that one of those guessing tricks turned out to leak secrets across process boundaries and broke a browser feature so badly it was switched off worldwide for two years.

What you can do at this point: say, with numbers, why waiting is the central problem, and why "just wait for the data" is not a neutral choice.

What's still broken: we have a fast machine and slow surroundings, and still no way to show a human anything.

Node 9 — the screen is just memory

The limitation: everything so far happens invisibly. Nothing has reached a person.

This node is short, because the answer is one sentence and it's the same sentence as Node 2's.

Set aside a region of the row of boxes and agree — a Node 4 agreement, existing nowhere but in the code — that every four consecutive boxes are one dot on the screen: how much red (0–255), how much green, how much blue, and one spare byte, often used for transparency. That region is the framebuffer. [EXPLAIN]

Separately, a piece of hardware reads that region continuously, top-left to bottom-right, and turns each group of four into a voltage on a cable going to the monitor. It does this whether or not anything changed, over and over, sixty times a second for a typical display. That's scanout.

So: drawing is writing numbers into boxes. There is no other kind of drawing anywhere in this document. Every glyph, every image, every rounded corner and shadow in every web page you have ever seen was, at the final step, some code writing values 0–255 into a run of boxes that hardware then read.

The numbers, because they matter in Node 37. A 1920×1080 screen has 1920 × 1080 = 2,073,600 dots, at 4 bytes each = 8,294,400 boxes, about 8.3 megabytes. Refreshed 60 times a second, the hardware reads about 500 megabytes per second just to keep the picture on the glass.

And 60 times a second means the hardware comes round for a fresh copy every 16.67 milliseconds — one sixtieth of a second. That number is not a target someone picked for smoothness; it's the reciprocal of a refresh rate that was itself inherited from the frequency of mains electricity in the era of cathode-ray televisions. It is the single most important number in the second half of this series, because it is the budget: whatever you want on screen has to be finished being written within 16.67 ms, or the hardware scans out the old picture again and the user sees a frame that didn't move. [EXPLAIN]

Two consequences worth planting now:

Tearing and double buffering. If you're rewriting the buffer while the hardware is reading it, the top of the screen shows new content and the bottom shows old — a visible horizontal seam. The fix is two buffers: draw into the one nobody's reading, then, in the tiny gap between one scanout finishing and the next starting, tell the hardware to read the other one instead. That gap is called the vertical blanking interval, and waiting for it is VSync. In Node 37 this is why requestAnimationFrame exists and why setTimeout(fn, 16) is a subtly wrong way to animate.

Why there's a GPU. Filling 8 million boxes with computed values, sixty times a second, using a CPU that does one thing at a time is a losing proposition. So machines grew a second processor with hundreds or thousands of small cores, each doing the same simple operation on different pixels simultaneously — genuine parallelism in the Node 7 sense. That's a GPU, its memory is where the framebuffer usually lives, and in Node 37 you'll find that the reason a page can keep scrolling smoothly while your JavaScript is stuck is that scrolling was arranged to be a GPU operation on an already-drawn layer, requiring nothing from the frozen thread.

What you can do at this point: show a human something, and know precisely what "show" means in cells.

What's still broken: everything this machine can display, it already had. Nothing here reaches another computer, and the entire subject of this series is a program whose whole purpose is to display things that live somewhere else.

Node 10 — talking to a machine you cannot touch

The limitation: a program can only work on boxes in its own row. Another machine's data is not at any address it can name.

Two machines on a desk share nothing: no boxes, no clock, no anything. A program on the left has no instruction that could read a byte from the right. The only connection you can make is physical — a wire between them — and Node 0 told us what a wire carries: on or off.

So the whole of networking is: agree on conventions for turning bytes into wire-wiggles and back, and then agree on conventions layered on top of those, and then more on top of those. Every layer exists because the layer below it left something broken. It's the same causal chain as everything else.

Layer one: bytes across a wire. A network card turns a byte into a timed sequence of voltage changes and the card at the other end turns them back. Add a rule for where a message starts and stops, and a checksum — a number computed from the message so the receiver can tell if it got mangled. This is Ethernet, designed by Bob Metcalfe at Xerox PARC in 1973. Two machines can now exchange chunks of bytes. [AWARE]

Layer two: more than two machines. With three machines you can't give everyone a wire to everyone; with a thousand it's absurd. So machines share a connection into a box that forwards traffic, and every card gets a permanent unique number burnt in at the factory (a MAC address) so a chunk can say who it's for.

But sharing raises a real question: if one machine sends a 4-gigabyte file, does everyone else wait an hour? The answer, and it's one of the genuinely great ideas, is packet switching — worked out independently by Paul Baran at RAND in 1964 and Donald Davies at the UK's National Physical Laboratory in 1965, who coined the word packet. Chop every message into small pieces, and send them interleaved with everyone else's. A typical maximum is 1500 bytes per packet on Ethernet. Nobody waits an hour; everybody's pieces take turns, and a big transfer is just a machine that has a lot of pieces queued. [EXPLAIN]

Layer three: machines that aren't on your wire. MAC addresses are flat — a factory-assigned number tells you nothing about where the machine is. So a second numbering was invented, this one hierarchical, like a postal system: an IP address, four bytes written as 142.250.183.14, where the leading part identifies a network and the rest identifies a machine within it. Now a packet can be handed to a router, which doesn't know where every machine on earth is — it only has to know which neighbour is "closer" to that leading part, and hand it on. Twenty-odd such hops later it arrives. IP is Vint Cerf and Bob Kahn's design from 1974; the internet formally switched to it on 1 January 1983. Four bytes gives 4.3 billion addresses, which ran out, which is why IPv6 exists with sixteen bytes. [EXPLAIN]

Layer four: making it reliable, because none of the above is. IP promises nothing. Packets can be lost when a router's queue is full, can arrive out of order because two took different paths, can be duplicated. Which is useless for fetching a document — you cannot render HTML with a chunk missing from the middle.

So TCP sits on top, and it's a convention with no new hardware at all. Number every chunk. The receiver sends back small acknowledgements saying what it has. The sender keeps a copy of everything unacknowledged and re-sends anything that goes unacknowledged too long. The receiver holds out-of-order pieces and hands them up in order. If acknowledgements come back slowly, the sender slows down, on the assumption that something along the path is congested. [EXPLAIN]

Two costs of that reliability, both of which shape the web:

  • The handshake. Before any data flows, the two ends exchange three small packets to agree they're both there and to set starting numbers. That's one full round trip before a single useful byte moves — Node 8's nineteen years, spent on hello. It's a large part of why fetching a small file from a distant server takes so much longer than the file's size suggests, and why every performance effort of the last decade has been about avoiding new connections.
  • Head-of-line blocking. Because bytes must be delivered in order, one lost packet stalls everything behind it until it's re-sent, even if the later data already arrived safely.

Layer five: which program on that machine? An IP address names a machine, but a machine runs many programs. So a two-byte number is added to each packet: a port, 0 to 65,535. A program tells the kernel "I want anything arriving for port 80," and the kernel routes accordingly. Some numbers are conventions everybody agreed on: 80 for web traffic, 443 for encrypted web traffic. There's nothing special about them; they're an agreed phone extension. [EXPLAIN]

And finally, what a program actually sees. All of that lives in the kernel, behind the doorway from Node 6. A program makes a system call saying "connect me to this address on this port." The kernel does the handshake and hands back a small integer — a file descriptor, an index into a table of the process's open connections. From then on, the program says "write these bytes to descriptor 7" and "read bytes from descriptor 7."

That's it. From inside a program, a connection to a machine on the other side of the world is a number you write bytes into and read bytes out of. [EXPLAIN] This design — Berkeley sockets, from BSD Unix in 1983 — is why the same code shape reads a file and talks to a server, and it's why "everything is a file" is a phrase people say about Unix.

One convenience on top: nobody wants to type 142.250.183.14. DNS, designed by Paul Mockapetris in 1983, is a distributed lookup that turns a name like info.cern.ch into an address — and it is itself a network request, so it costs a round trip of its own before your connection can even begin. [USE]

Now stack it all up, because this is the thing a browser does before it has a single byte of a page:

  1. Ask DNS for the address behind the name. One round trip.
  2. TCP handshake with that address. One round trip.
  3. (In practice, an encryption handshake too — Node 33. One or two more.)
  4. Send the request. Half a round trip.
  5. Receive the answer. Half a round trip.

Three or four round trips before the first byte of HTML exists in memory. At Node 8's 200 ms across the world, that's most of a second, of which almost none is transferring data. The web's entire performance history is people fighting these round trips.

What you can do at this point: open a connection to a machine anywhere on earth, write bytes to it, read bytes back — and know exactly how expensive that is, and why.

What's still broken: you can move bytes between two machines that already agree on what the bytes mean. There is no agreement. There is no way to say which document you want, no format for a document, and no notion of a document pointing at another one. Two machines can talk and have nothing to say.

Part I is complete. You now have, built from a wire: numbers, memory as numbered boxes, a chip that reads its own instructions, meaning as pure convention, the stack that makes function calls possible, an operating system with privilege and interrupts and private memory worlds, threads as positions with all their hazards, the exact cost of the outside world, a screen that is memory, and a connection that is a number you write bytes into.

Everything in Parts II through V is built only from those. Nothing new gets smuggled in.

PART II — THE DOCUMENT

Node 11 — the problem that existed before the web

The limitation: Node 10 ended with two machines able to move bytes and nothing to say. That's not a hypothetical. That was the actual state of the world in 1989, and it was miserable in a specific way that a specific person got specifically annoyed by.

The place is CERN, the particle physics laboratory straddling the French–Swiss border outside Geneva. About ten thousand people are associated with it. Very few of them are permanent — most are physicists from universities elsewhere, on contracts of a year or two, who arrive, join an experiment, learn how a detector and its software work, contribute, and leave. Then somebody new arrives and has to learn the same things again.

And the machines are a zoo. IBM mainframes, DEC VAXes, Unix workstations from four vendors, Macs, PCs — different in every respect that matters: different text encodings (Node 4's conventions, chosen differently), different file systems, different networking, different document formats from different word processors that could not read each other's files.

So the question "how does this piece of equipment work?" resolves, in practice, into a chain: who wrote that down → which machine are they on → do I have an account there → what format is it in → do I have anything that reads that format → and is this the current version or the one from two years ago? Every answer requires a human, and the humans keep leaving.

Tim Berners-Lee, a software engineer there, had already taken one run at this. In 1980, during an earlier contract, he wrote a private program called Enquire — named after a Victorian household manual, Enquire Within Upon Everything — which stored notes with links between them: this person works on that project, that project uses this machine. It worked, for him, on one machine. And that was the ceiling: the links only pointed at things inside his own database, so it couldn't describe anything belonging to anyone else.

What already existed, and why none of it was the answer

This is the part people skip, and skipping it makes the web look like it appeared in a vacuum. It didn't. By 1989 the network from Node 10 was twenty years old and had plenty on top of it.

FTP (1971) moved files between machines. It solved transport, and only transport. You had to already know the machine, the path and the format, you needed an account, and having fetched a file you had a copy that immediately began going stale. [AWARE]

Email and Usenet moved text between people, brilliantly. But a message is a message — it goes to whoever it was sent to, it isn't a document that persists somewhere with an identity, and you cannot point at a paragraph of one from somewhere else.

Telnet let you log in to a distant machine and use it directly — so you saw its documents in its own format on its own terms, and you needed an account on every machine you cared about.

Gopher, from the University of Minnesota, arrived in 1991 and was for a while more popular than the web. It gave you a menu of items on a server; pick one and you get a document, or another menu. Clean, and structurally a tree — a hierarchy of menus. Everything lives in one place under one parent, and a document cannot point sideways at another document on another server. (Gopher also died for a second, dumber reason: in 1993 the university announced it would charge licensing fees for the server software, and universities fled. CERN had put the web in the public domain two months earlier.)

So the state of the art was: you could fetch a thing if you already knew everything about it, or browse somebody's menu. What did not exist anywhere was a document that could contain a pointer to any other document, anywhere, on any machine, and a program that would follow that pointer for you.

The idea that was missing, which wasn't new either

The concept had been sitting around for forty years without an implementation that stuck.

In July 1945, Vannevar Bush published an essay in The Atlantic called "As We May Think," describing an imaginary desk-sized machine he called the memex, which stored documents on microfilm and — the key part — let a reader build associative trails between them, so that one document could be linked to another and the trail followed later.

In 1965, Ted Nelson gave the idea a name: hypertext. Text that is not a straight line, because it contains links.

In December 1968, Douglas Engelbart gave a demonstration in San Francisco that has been called the Mother of All Demos, showing a working system with linked documents, a mouse, and two people editing the same document over a network. In 1968.

So by 1989 the concept was old, well-named, and repeatedly built. Every implementation had the same ceiling as Enquire: links only worked within one system, because a link had to identify its target, and there was no way to identify something on a machine you didn't control.

The memo

In March 1989, Berners-Lee circulated a document titled Information Management: A Proposal. His manager Mike Sendall wrote on the cover the phrase that ended up in every history of the web: "Vague, but exciting." Nothing happened for a year. In May 1990 he revised it, now with Robert Cailliau; in September 1990 he got permission to buy a machine and try it.

What made the proposal work where forty years of prior art hadn't was not any single technical idea. It was a choice about failure, and it's worth stating precisely because it recurs in every node from here on:

Links are allowed to be broken, and no machine is in charge.

Every serious hypertext system before it tried to guarantee link integrity — a link should never point at nothing — which requires the system to know about, and have authority over, both ends. That's achievable within one organisation and impossible across the world.

Berners-Lee gave that up. A link is just a name written down. Nobody checks it when you write it. Nobody is told when the target moves. Follow a link to something that's gone and you get an error, and that's fine. In exchange, you need permission from nobody to link to anything, and any machine can join by simply answering requests. [EXPLAIN]

That trade — correctness surrendered for permissionless growth — is why the web scaled and why 404 exists. And you will watch the same trade get made again in Node 14, when parsers choose to render broken documents rather than reject them, and again in Node 39, when the standards process gives up on telling browsers what to do and starts writing down what they already did.

What you can do at this point: name the exact gap — no way to identify a document on a machine you don't control, and therefore no way to point at one.

What's still broken: all of it. There is a proposal and no software.

Node 12 — three inventions in one memo: the URL, HTTP, HTML

The limitation: you need to be able to say which document, ask for it, and receive it in a form that can contain pointers to others.

Three separate problems, three separate inventions. People blur them into "the web," and keeping them apart is most of what makes the rest of this series legible.

Invention one: a name for every document anywhere

The problem, precisely: from Node 10, to fetch something you must know a machine and then, somehow, tell that machine which of its documents you want. Two different pieces of information, expressed in two different systems, plus a third — which protocol to speak, since FTP and the new thing aren't the same conversation.

The move is to jam all of it into one string with a fixed grammar:

http://info.cern.ch:80/hypertext/WWW/TheProject.html
└─┬─┘   └──────┬─────┘└┬┘└────────────┬────────────┘
scheme      host      port          path
  • scheme — which conversation to have. http here; ftp, mailto and others were included from the start, because it was designed to name things that already existed, not just new things.
  • host — a name that DNS (Node 10) turns into an IP address.
  • port — which program on that machine (Node 10's two-byte number). Omitted almost always, because http defaults to 80, a number Berners-Lee simply picked from the unassigned range.
  • path — a string the server interprets. This is the subtle bit: to your browser, the path is opaque text. It doesn't mean a file. It means whatever the machine at the other end decides it means. [EXPLAIN]

Originally called a UDI — Universal Document Identifier — and standardised in December 1994 as the URL, Uniform Resource Locator. The renaming from Universal to Uniform was, apparently, a committee finding "universal" arrogant.

What this buys is the thing forty years of hypertext lacked: a document on one machine can contain a name that unambiguously identifies a document on any other machine, without either machine's cooperation. The link no longer needs a system that knows both ends. It needs a string. [EXPLAIN]

Invention two: how to ask

Node 10 left you with a connection: a number you write bytes into and read bytes out of. So a protocol is just an agreement about which bytes.

The first version — retroactively named HTTP/0.9 — is so small you can hold all of it:

  1. Open a TCP connection to the host on port 80.
  2. Write these ASCII bytes: GET /hypertext/WWW/TheProject.html followed by a carriage return and a line feed.
  3. Read bytes until the server closes the connection. Those bytes are the document.

That's the entire protocol. No headers. No status codes. No other verbs. No way to say what format you'd like or what format you're getting. The end of the document is signalled by the connection being closed, which means one connection per document — and, from Node 10, a fresh TCP handshake and its full round trip for every single file. [EXPLAIN]

Two properties were baked in here that turned out to matter more than anything else in the design.

It's text. The request is human-readable ASCII, which meant anyone could test a server by typing at it, and any language on any machine could speak it without a special library. Compare a binary protocol where you must get byte offsets exactly right to send anything at all. Cheap participation was the whole strategy.

It's stateless. The server answers the request and forgets you exist. It keeps nothing between requests. This is why a server can handle enormous numbers of clients — it has no per-client memory to maintain — and it is also, precisely, why logging in required inventing something extra later, and why that something is cookies, and why cookies caused the security problem that Node 34 is entirely about. [EXPLAIN]

Invention three: a format that can contain links

You need a document format that (a) any machine can render, (b) says what its parts are, and (c) can hold a URL pointing elsewhere.

Rather than invent from nothing, he borrowed a shape already in use at CERN. SGML — a 1986 ISO standard, descended from work at IBM in 1969 — is a system for marking up text with tags in angle brackets, where a tag says what a piece of text is rather than what it looks like. <title>, <paragraph>, <heading>.

HTML is a small, fixed set of such tags. The first description, circulated around late 1991, lists eighteen of them. Thirteen of those eighteen still exist today, which is a remarkable survival rate for anything in computing.

And one of the eighteen is the entire point of the exercise:

<a href="http://info.cern.ch/hypertext/WWW/TheProject.html">the project</a>

An anchor, whose href — hypertext reference — holds a URL from invention one. Text you can act on, carrying a name for something elsewhere. That's the web. Everything else in HTML is supporting cast. [EXPLAIN]

Note what HTML deliberately does not say: it doesn't say how big the heading is, what font, what colour. That's left to the program displaying it — which was essential in 1990, when the displaying program might be a graphical workstation or a text terminal with 24 lines and no fonts at all. This separation of what a thing is from how it looks is why a page can be read aloud by a screen reader, or restyled entirely by a stylesheet, and it's the principle the industry then spent 1995–2000 comprehensively violating with <font> and <center> before rediscovering it. [EXPLAIN]

The three together

Watch them interlock, because the elegance is the reason it beat everything else:

HTML documents contain URLs. Following one means using its scheme to pick a protocol — usually HTTP — and its host to find a machine and its path to name the document. The document that comes back is more HTML, containing more URLs.

The loop closes. Any document can point at any document. No registry, no authority, no permission, no coordination — because a URL is a self-contained name that requires nobody's agreement to write down.

What you can do at this point: name a document anywhere, ask for it, and receive something that can point onward.

What's still broken: all three are specifications — paper. Somebody has to write the program.

Node 13 — what the first browser actually was, as a program

The limitation: conventions on paper move no bytes.

In September 1990 Berners-Lee got a NeXT machine — a black magnesium cube, 25 MHz, 8 megabytes of memory, running a Unix-derived operating system of the kind Node 6 describes. Between October and December 1990 he wrote a program on it called WorldWideWeb, later renamed Nexus to stop it colliding with the name of the thing itself. On 20 December 1990 the first web page went up on info.cern.ch.

Here is that program, entirely, in terms of Part I. This is the definition of "browser" and it never changes; everything in the remaining twenty-six nodes is an elaboration of one of these four steps.

Step one — turn the URL into a connection. Take the string apart at the punctuation. Hand the host portion to DNS, which is itself a network request (Node 10) costing a round trip, and get back four bytes of IP address. Make a system call (Node 6) asking the kernel to open a TCP connection to that address on port 80. The kernel does the three-packet handshake, another round trip. It hands back a small integer — a file descriptor (Node 10).

Step two — write the request. Build the bytes GET /path\r\n — which, from Node 4, means putting the numbers 71, 69, 84, 32, 47… into a run of boxes. Make a system call: write these bytes to descriptor 7.

Step three — read the answer. Make a system call: read from descriptor 7 into this buffer, a region of boxes the program set aside. The kernel parks the thread (Node 7) — no CPU burnt, but this thread is doing nothing at all for the Node 8 round-trip time, tens or hundreds of milliseconds, hundreds of millions of instructions' worth. Bytes arrive; the kernel wakes the thread; it reads more, until the server closes the connection. Now the document exists as numbers in the program's own memory.

Step four — turn those bytes into pixels. Interpret the buffer as text under an agreed encoding (Node 4), find the angle brackets, work out the structure, decide where everything goes, and write red-green-blue values into the framebuffer region (Node 9) so the scanout hardware puts it on the glass.

A browser is a program that turns bytes from a socket into numbers in the framebuffer. [EXPLAIN]

That's it. That is the whole job description, in 1990 and today. A modern browser is thirty million lines of code because step four grew a rendering engine, a programming language, a security model and a compiler, and because step two grew defences against everything people tried to do with it — but the four steps are the same four steps.

Two things about that first program worth carrying forward

It was also an editor. WorldWideWeb didn't just display pages; you could edit them in place and save them back. Berners-Lee's model was that reading and writing the web were the same activity, and he has said for decades that losing this was the great mistake. It got lost for a boring reason: the browsers that followed were much easier to write if they only read, editing required a way to write back to a server that HTTP/0.9 didn't have, and by the time the protocol could, browsing had become a read-only habit. The web spent twenty years re-growing writing as forms submitted to programs (Node 17) — which is a far more complicated thing than editing a document, and is the entire reason web applications exist as a category.

It only ran on NeXT machines, of which almost none existed. So in 1991 Nicola Pellow wrote the line-mode browser, which worked on any terminal — no graphics, no mouse, links shown as numbers you typed. Ugly, and the reason the web could spread at all, because almost everyone's access to a computer in 1991 was a text terminal.

And then the decision that did more than any other: on 30 April 1993, CERN put the software into the public domain. Anyone could implement it, use it, sell it, with no fee and no permission. Two months later Minnesota announced Gopher licensing fees. That contrast is a large part of why you are reading this in a browser.

What you can do at this point: fetch a document from any machine on earth and get it into your own memory as bytes.

What's still broken: step four was one sentence pretending to be simple. "Work out the structure" is doing enormous work. You have a run of forty thousand boxes holding numbers that mean ASCII characters, some of which are angle brackets. That is not a document — it is flat, and the thing it describes is nested. Nothing so far can turn one into the other.

Next: Node 14 — from a run of bytes to a tree in memory, where the parser gets built as a state machine, the DOM turns out to be blocks of boxes holding each other's addresses, and browsers make the second great "accept broken input" trade of this series.

Node 14 — from a run of bytes to a tree in memory

The limitation: the document arrived as a flat run of boxes holding numbers that mean characters. Flat is wrong. What the characters describe is nested.

A paragraph sits inside a section. A bold word sits inside the paragraph. A cell sits inside a row inside a table. The bytes are in address order, one after another, with no structure at all — structure is only implied by the angle brackets, and implication is not something a program can act on.

So the first real job is: build, in your own memory, a structure with the same shape as the thing the bytes describe.

Why a tree, and what a tree is in boxes

The shape is a tree because containment has exactly the properties of a tree: everything sits inside exactly one thing, and things nest without ever partially overlapping. You never have a paragraph that starts inside a table cell and ends outside it. (People write that, and Node 14's second half is entirely about what happens then.)

Now — what is a tree, in the row of numbered boxes from Node 2? There's no "tree" in memory any more than there's a letter. There are numbers. So you make a Node 4 agreement.

Take one element, say a paragraph. Set aside a block of consecutive boxes and agree that:

  • the first few boxes hold a number saying what kind of node this is (say 1 for element, 3 for text)
  • the next eight hold the address of the block for this node's parent
  • the next eight hold the address of the block for its first child
  • the next eight hold the address of the block for its next sibling
  • the next eight hold the address of a separate block listing its attributes
  • and for a text node, the address of a run of boxes holding the characters

That's a DOM node. A block of boxes whose fields are mostly the addresses of other blocks of boxes. [EXPLAIN] Eight boxes per address because modern machines use 64-bit addresses (Node 2), and 64 bits is 8 bytes.

Note what a "tree" then physically is: blocks scattered anywhere in memory, in no particular order, tied together only by the numbers they hold about each other. Draw it on paper and it's a neat tree; in memory it's confetti with addresses written on it.

Why hold addresses instead of just laying children out consecutively after their parent? Because the structure has to change. Insert a paragraph in the middle of a page laid out consecutively and you must shift millions of boxes along to make room. Insert it into the address-linked version and you write three numbers: the new block's next-sibling field, the previous sibling's next-sibling field, and the parent's first-child field if it went at the front. Three writes, a handful of instructions, regardless of document size. [EXPLAIN]

And now something you've written a thousand times is fully explained. document.body.firstChild.nextSibling reads the first-child address out of body's block, goes to that block, reads its next-sibling address, goes to that block. Three memory reads, a few hundred instructions in Node 8's terms — genuinely cheap. Whereas document.querySelectorAll('.item') must walk the entire confetti, following every address, checking every node's attributes. That's why one is free and the other isn't, and it isn't a rule about "the DOM being slow." It's what the two operations are made of.

One name to place: DOM stands for Document Object Model, and it is the agreed interface to this structure — the set of names and operations (firstChild, appendChild, parentNode) that a browser promises to offer. It was first standardised as DOM Level 1 in October 1998, after Netscape and Microsoft had each invented incompatible versions. The tree exists in memory whether or not anything ever asks about it; the DOM is the vocabulary for asking. In Node 18 that distinction becomes the whole story, because giving a program access to that vocabulary is what turns a document into an application. [EXPLAIN]

The parser, as a state machine

Now: how do you get from characters to blocks?

You read one character at a time and keep a variable saying what you're in the middle of. That variable is the state, and a program shaped like this is a state machine. [EXPLAIN] From Node 3 it's unremarkable — a number in a register, and a conditional jump that goes to different code depending on its value.

state = TEXT
for each character:
    if state is TEXT:
        if char is '<'  -> state = TAG_NAME, start collecting a name
        else            -> add char to the text being accumulated
    if state is TAG_NAME:
        if char is a space -> state = ATTR_NAME
        if char is '>'     -> the tag is complete; emit it
        else               -> add char to the name
    if state is ATTR_NAME:
        ...

Each complete tag or run of text that comes out is a token, and this half is the tokeniser. The real HTML tokeniser has around eighty states, because of quoted and unquoted attribute values, comments, doctypes, CDATA, script contents, and character references — but every one of them is this same shape.

Then a second stage turns the stream of tokens into the tree, and it needs to remember where it currently is in the nesting. It does that with a list it only ever adds to and removes from at one end — push an element when you see an opening tag, pop it when you see the matching closing tag. The element on the end is "the thing I'm currently inside," so any new node gets attached as its child.

Careful here, because two different things share a word. In Node 5, "the stack" meant a specific region of memory the CPU uses for function calls, with the stack pointer register. What we have now is a data structure used in the same disciplined way — add and remove at one end only — but it's an ordinary list in ordinary memory, built by the parser for its own bookkeeping. The word "stack" describes the discipline, not the location. The parser's stack of open elements and the CPU's call stack are unrelated things that happen to both be last-in-first-out. [EXPLAIN]

The decision that shaped the industry

What should the parser do when the document is wrong? And documents are wrong constantly — <b><i>hello</b></i>, a <p> never closed, an attribute value missing its closing quote, a </div> too many.

The clean engineering answer is: refuse. Report a parse error. That is what XML chose, and what XHTML tried to force onto the web around 2000, and it is why XHTML lost.

The answer browsers actually took, from the earliest ones and hardened for thirty years, is:

Never fail. Always produce a tree.

Because a parse error shown to a reader is useless. They didn't write the page, can't fix it, and don't care whose fault it is — they just see a broken browser and use a different one. A browser that renders the sloppy page beats a browser that is right about it, every single time. [EXPLAIN]

This is the same trade as Node 11's broken links, made again: give up correctness, win adoption.

And it has a very long tail. "Be lenient" is not a specification. So through the nineties every browser was lenient differently — one closed your unclosed <p> when it hit a <div>, another nested it — and identical bytes produced different trees in Netscape and Internet Explorer, which is the technical root of "works in one browser, broken in the other" and of a decade of writing every page twice.

The eventual fix, in the HTML5 work from roughly 2004 through 2012, was strange and correct: rather than defining what valid HTML means and leaving errors undefined, the specification defines exactly what to do for every possible malformed input — a big machine of "insertion modes" with precise rules for every unexpected token in every state. There's even a named procedure, the adoption agency algorithm, whose sole job is untangling misnested formatting tags like that <b><i>hello</b></i>. Thousands of lines of pure error recovery, written down so all browsers get the same wrong answer. [AWARE] The web's specifications largely stopped saying what pages should be and started saying what browsers must do.

Three more things the parser has to handle

It runs on bytes as they arrive, not on the whole document. From Node 10, the response comes in packets — 1500 bytes at a time, spread over Node 8's slow round trips. Waiting for the last one before starting would mean the screen stays blank until the whole thing lands. So the parser is incremental: it consumes whatever has arrived, builds what it can, and resumes when more comes. This is why you see a page appear progressively rather than all at once. [EXPLAIN]

It has to guess the encoding before it can read anything. Node 4: bytes are only characters by convention, and you must know which convention. But the declaration of the convention — <meta charset="utf-8"> — is itself inside the document, written in that convention. So browsers read the first chunk assuming something ASCII-compatible, hunt for a declaration in roughly the first kilobyte, and if they find a different one, throw away the tree so far and start again. If they find nothing, they guess from statistics and locale. That is the entire origin of pages full of â€™ — a guess that went the other way. [USE]

Some characters can't be written literally. < starts a tag, so a document that wants a literal less-than sign needs an escape hatch: &lt;. Which makes & special too, so it needs &amp;. These are character references, and the double-escaping bugs you've seen — &amp;amp; displayed on a page — are exactly one round of this applied twice.

What you can do at this point: hold a document in memory as a structure a program can walk, from any input, however broken.

What's still broken: a tree of blocks holding each other's addresses is not something anyone can see. Not one pixel has been written.

Node 15 — from a tree to pixels

The limitation: the tree describes structure; the screen from Node 9 is a grid of coloured dots. Nothing connects them.

Three passes, in order. Most of it is arithmetic.

Pass one: style — decide what each node should look like

Each node needs values: font family, size, colour, spacing, whether it stacks vertically or flows inline.

The first browsers hardcoded this — an <h1> was big and bold because the code said so. Every browser still has this as its floor: a built-in default stylesheet, which is why unstyled HTML isn't invisible, and why headings are bold and links are blue-and-underlined in a page with no CSS at all.

Authors wanted control immediately, and the first answer was to put appearance in the markup: <font color="red">, <center>, bgcolor. This works, and it is a disaster, because appearance is now welded to structure — restyling a thousand pages means editing a thousand pages, and the same paragraph can't be styled differently in two contexts.

CSS breaks the weld. Proposed by Håkon Wium Lie on 10 October 1994 (with Bert Bos; a W3C Recommendation in December 1996), its idea is: rules live separately and select nodes by what they are or what class they carry.

p.intro { font-size: 18px; color: #333; }

The browser matches rules against nodes and computes, for every node, a final value for every property. Which needs two extra mechanisms, both of which trip people constantly and both of which are just conflict resolution:

The cascade. Several rules can match one node, and they can disagree. So there's an ordering: rules from the browser's default stylesheet lose to rules from the page, later rules beat earlier ones, and more specific selectors beat vaguer ones — p.intro beats p. Specificity is literally a count of the id, class and element parts of the selector, compared piecewise. That's the whole algorithm, and it's why an id selector cannot be overridden by ten class selectors: it's not a weight, it's a higher column. [USE]

Inheritance. Some properties, if unset, take the parent's computed value — colour and font do, because you want to set them once on the body; borders and margins don't, because a border on every descendant would be absurd. Which properties inherit is a fixed per-property fact in the spec, not a rule you can derive. [USE]

The output of this pass is, per node, a complete set of values with nothing left unspecified. [EXPLAIN]

Pass two: layout — decide where everything goes

Now the arithmetic. Every node gets four numbers: x, y, width, height — a rectangle. The coordinate system has (0,0) at the top-left, x increasing rightward, and y increasing downward, which is a convention inherited from Node 9's scanout hardware sweeping the screen top to bottom, not from mathematics.

The interesting part is that layout is two directions at once, and this explains most layout behaviour that feels arbitrary:

Width flows down. A block element's width is determined by its parent's width — the parent knows how much room it has and hands it to the child. So width can be computed on the way in, top to bottom.

Height flows up. A paragraph's height depends on how many lines its text takes, which depends on the words, the font, and the width it was given. So the child computes its own height and reports it back, and only then does the parent know its own height, and only then does the parent's parent know. Height is computed on the way out, bottom to top. [EXPLAIN]

That asymmetry is why width: 100% is easy and "make this child fill the remaining height" was hard for twenty years until flexbox and grid gave the parent a way to do a second pass.

Inside a block, text is laid out by line breaking: measure each word's width in the chosen font, add it to the current line, and when the line exceeds the available width, start a new one. Each line is one font-height tall. Count the lines, multiply, and you have the paragraph's height. Genuinely: measure, add, compare, wrap.

This whole pass has a name in engine source code — layout, or reflow — and its cost is roughly proportional to how many nodes must be touched. [EXPLAIN]

And now the property that becomes the central performance fact of the browser: layout is not local. Change one element's width and every following sibling may move, which changes the parent's height, which moves everything after the parent. One small change can invalidate the geometry of the entire page. That coupling is the seed of Node 37, and the reason "just move this one thing" can cost more than it looks.

Pass three: paint — write the numbers

Every node now has a rectangle, a colour, a font. So walk them and write bytes into the pixel buffer from Node 9.

Fill a rectangle: a loop writing four bytes per dot. Draw a character: fonts are stored as outlines — mathematical curves describing the shape of each glyph — which get rasterised, meaning the curves are evaluated to work out which dots they cover and how much, producing a small greyscale bitmap that is then blended into the buffer at the right position in the right colour. Rasterised glyphs are cached, because you draw the letter "e" thousands of times per page.

Order matters, because things overlap and later writes cover earlier ones. So painting follows a defined sequence — backgrounds, then borders, then text, then anything positioned or z-indexed, in a defined stacking order. When two elements overlap and the wrong one is on top, this pass is where the answer lives.

In practice modern engines add a step: rather than painting everything into one buffer, they paint into several layers which the GPU then combines. That's what makes it possible to move something on screen without repainting anything — Node 37's subject entirely.

Putting the whole path together

For the first view of a page: bytes arrive over the slow connection (Node 10) → parsed incrementally into a tree of address-linked blocks (Node 14) → style computed per node → layout assigns rectangles → paint writes numbers into a buffer → the scanout hardware puts it on the glass (Node 9), 60 times a second, every 16.67 milliseconds.

That's a complete browser: fetch, parse, style, layout, paint. It is 1993, and it works.

What you can do at this point: display any document from any machine on earth.

What's still broken: the page is a photograph. It was computed once, from bytes fetched once. Nothing in it can change. The only way to see anything different is to fetch a whole new document and destroy this one.

Node 16 — the browser people actually used, and the tag that changed everything

The limitation: the software existed and almost nobody could run it.

WorldWideWeb ran only on NeXT machines, of which very few existed. The line-mode browser ran anywhere and was text-only. Meanwhile several people built graphical browsers — ViolaWWW, Erwise, MidasWWW — mostly for Unix workstations, mostly by one or two people.

Then, in February 1993, the National Center for Supercomputing Applications at the University of Illinois released Mosaic, written by Marc Andreessen and Eric Bina. Three things made it the one that mattered.

It ran on machines people had. Unix first, then Windows and Mac later in 1993. That was the whole game: in 1993 the graphical web existed only for people with an expensive workstation, and Mosaic put it on office PCs.

It installed easily, with no compilation and no Unix expertise.

And it put images in the page. On 25 February 1993, Andreessen proposed the <img> tag on the www-talk mailing list. Berners-Lee didn't like it — it named one specific medium instead of a general mechanism for including any kind of resource, and there was a competing proposal to do the general thing. The general thing was better designed. Andreessen shipped <img> anyway, and it was in the browser everyone was using, so it won.

That is the first appearance of a pattern that runs through the next twenty-five years, and it's worth naming since it explains most of the weirdness in the platform: on the web, shipping beats specifying. A feature that exists in a browser people use becomes real, and standards bodies follow along afterwards writing down what happened. Node 39 is where that becomes an explicit policy.

Andreessen left NCSA, co-founded a company, and Netscape Navigator shipped in late 1994. Within a year it had the overwhelming majority of users. Microsoft, having nearly missed the internet entirely, licensed the Mosaic code from a company called Spyglass and shipped Internet Explorer 1.0 in August 1995, bundled with Windows — and the browser war began, which is Node 39's story.

For now, only the consequence matters: from 1994 onward, browser features were shipped as weapons in a commercial fight, fast, unilaterally, and incompatibly. Almost every strange corner of the platform dates from this period, including the entire subject of Part III.

What you can do at this point: millions of ordinary people can use the web.

What's still broken: they can only read. The web is a library, and everyone wants it to be a shop, a bank, a mail client.

Node 17 — forms, CGI, and the wound

The limitation: the document is a photograph, and a photograph cannot take input.

The mechanism added was the form: <form> wrapping <input> fields and a submit button. Press submit, and the browser gathers every field's name and value, encodes them as name=value&name=value with special characters percent-escaped, and sends them to the URL in the form's action.

Two ways to send them, and the distinction is exactly this:

  • GET glues the encoded pairs onto the URL after a ?. So the whole request is a URL — bookmarkable, shareable, and re-sent identically if you reload. Which is right for a search and wrong for a payment.
  • POST puts them in a request body — bytes sent after the request headers. Not in the URL, not bookmarkable, and the browser warns before re-sending on reload. Right for anything that changes something. [USE]

(A request body needs a place to put it, which HTTP/0.9 from Node 12 didn't have. Forms are one of the reasons HTTP grew headers and a body — the version that has them, HTTP/1.0, was written down in 1996.)

On the other end, the server has to do something with those values, which means running a program rather than reading a file. CGI — the Common Gateway Interface, from the NCSA server in 1993 — defined the dumbest possible arrangement that works: when a request arrives for a certain path, start a program (a whole new process, Node 6), hand it the submitted values, and send whatever it prints back as the response. [AWARE] That's the origin of server-side programming as a job, and of every framework that followed.

And now the wound, which is the reason for everything in Parts III and IV

Look at the shape of the cycle this creates. Every interaction is:

  1. Discard the entire page — the tree from Node 14, all of it, gone.
  2. Open a connection and ask the server for a whole new page. Node 10: DNS, handshake, request, response. Node 8: a hundred-odd milliseconds at best.
  3. Wait.
  4. Parse the new document from scratch. Style it from scratch. Lay it out from scratch. Paint it from scratch.

Every one. Tick a checkbox that should reveal one extra field — full cycle. Get one field wrong in a twelve-field form — full cycle, and the page that comes back has often lost what you typed, because the server must re-render every value from its own memory and frequently didn't bother. Sort a table by a different column — full cycle.

And in 1995 the connection was a modem: 28.8 kilobits per second, about 3.6 kilobytes a second. A modest 40-kilobyte page is eleven seconds. The screen goes blank, then white, then redraws, and the thing you were reading is gone.

Put Node 8's numbers on it. During each of those waits, the chip in front of the user — running at three billion instructions a second — executes essentially nothing. It has the document tree sitting in its own memory, it has the framebuffer, it has all the capability built through ten nodes of Part I, and it is idle, because the document is a photograph and the only permitted action is to request a different photograph from a machine on another continent.

That is the wound. And note precisely what's wrong: it isn't that the network is slow. It's that the user's own machine is not allowed to do anything at all, so every change of any size must pay the full network cost.

What you can do at this point: build a real interactive application, as long as the user waits for a round trip on every single interaction.

What's still broken: there is no program running inside the page. Nothing on the user's machine can look at the tree, decide anything, or change one word on the screen without asking a distant machine for permission.
